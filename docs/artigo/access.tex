\begin{abstract}
A fixed substation-inspection patrol does not adapt to what the robot senses; a purely
reactive controller answers only stimuli already in range. We bridge both with a
two-layer architecture: a deliberative \emph{active-sensing} patrol (ROS~2, Nav2,
MPPI) carries the sensors to where events may occur, and a reactive layer maps sensory
saliency to an attraction/repulsion field driving a threshold-triggered finite-state
controller that approaches anomalies and retreats from hazards, hazards taking
precedence when eligible. We evaluate it in a simulated substation (Gazebo Fortress)
against a fixed patrol, a potential-field baseline and a
detect--select--deviate--return heuristic on the \emph{same} Nav2/MPPI controller,
isolating the field from deviating itself. (i)~On a \emph{static} hazard on the patrol
route, a fixed patrol with the same detection-triggered keep-out matched the ablation
that keeps the reactive inspection ($1.73$ vs.\ $1.71$~m median): \emph{the safety
benefit here is attributable to the deliberative keep-out, not to the reactive
weighting}, the reactive layer's contribution being the inspections.
(ii)~Replacing saliency by proximity changed nothing detectably, whereas replacing the
thresholded field by raw detection---which also removes the hazard channel's temporal
persistence---halved the clearance to a hazard co-located with a stronger anomaly and
delayed the priority inspection. (iii)~That precedence costs inspections: a marginally
eligible hazard suppresses attraction and forfeits one of four. Inspection
completeness degrades under high perception noise and dense clustering. Perception is
abstracted to oracle events and localization is fixed: the results speak to the
decision logic, not a deployable system. Code, world, data and scripts are released.
\end{abstract}
